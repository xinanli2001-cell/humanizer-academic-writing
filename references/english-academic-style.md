# English academic writing guidance

## Venue and variety

Follow the explicit requirements and style guide of the target venue, including the target journal, conference, school, or publisher, first. Otherwise preserve the source document's established variety and house practice. For new prose with no contrary signal, use British/Australian English. Keep spelling, punctuation, capitalisation, terminology, and citation style internally consistent.

## Register and precision

Aim for roughly 30% conversational expression and 70% academic expression as a reading impression, not a quota of words or sentences. Keep definitions, methods, data, and evidence claims precise; use everyday phrasing in explanations and examples. Preserve the author's supported judgements and individual voice. Allow clear elliptical additions and asymmetrical constructions, with sentence lengths varying naturally. Avoid over-regular syntax and mechanically divided paragraphs. Remove filler, stock phrases, and repeated summaries without inventing personal experience or weakening the argument. Explicit venue requirements remain governed by the main skill's priority rules.

Use direct, discipline-appropriate prose and prefer simple, precise verbs where they express the intended meaning. Retain technical or formally justified terms, and do not synonym-cycle stable terminology merely to create surface variation. First person, passive voice, nominalisation, hedging, and signposting are acceptable when required by the field, venue, evidence strength, information structure, or a clear rhetorical purpose.

Prefer active voice, familiar words, precise verbs, and concrete examples supported by the materials. Develop one main point per paragraph, stating it first and then supplying evidence and details. Do not invent an agent to obtain active voice. End on a supported fact or judgement, without a summary-style closing sentence or rhetorical elevation. Do not use self-posed question-and-answer packaging in newly written prose.

## Sentences and paragraphs

Let sentence and paragraph length follow the density of the argument and evidence. Remove empty announcements, repetitive topic sentences, promotional framing, generic conclusions, and rhythmic fragments only when they lack content or rhetorical function. Preserve deliberate parallelism, genuine three-part findings, and useful repeated openings. Do not split a coherent argument, repeat information, or add filler merely to manufacture variation.

## Formulaic prose checks

Apply these checks within the task's editing permissions. Treat the expressions below as contextual editing prompts, not authorship evidence or a global replacement list. Preserve quotations, established terms, source meaning, and venue requirements.

The user's explicit lexical restriction takes precedence over the watch-list treatment below: do not use `delve` or `leverage` in newly written or authorised rewritten narration. Express the intended meaning precisely in other words. Preserve protected quotations, titles, identifiers, and text the user explicitly requires unchanged under the main skill's semantic-lock rules.

- End a paragraph when its argument is complete. Omit appended wrappers such as `Conclusion:`, `In short: …`, and `The simplest mental model is: …` when they merely repeat the preceding point. Retain required Conclusion sections and substantive synthesis of findings.
- State the finding directly instead of announcing it with `it's worth noting` or `what's important is`. Remove `really` and `truly` when they add emphasis without changing meaning. Keep qualifications that express actual uncertainty or scope.
- Avoid rhetorical `Question? Answer.` packaging, invented objections, and empty contrasts such as `This isn't about X. It's about Y`, `X, not Y`, or `X—not Y`. Preserve actual research questions, source question-and-answer material, and distinctions required by the evidence. Describe the method, claim, or planned action directly; retain informative exclusions, negative findings, and limitations.
- Prefer a precise action to vague uses of `promote`: name what was examined, changed, or used. Review `unlock`, `navigate`, `embrace`, `elevate`, `revolutionize`, `groundbreaking`, `game-changing`, `robust`, `landscape`, `paradigm`, `ecosystem`, `journey`, and `roadmap` for concrete referents and evidence. Delete empty slogans such as `in this digital age`, `at the end of the day`, and `think outside the box`. Retain meaningful disciplinary uses, including robust estimation, biological promotion, and ecological ecosystems; do not substitute synonyms just to avoid a watched word.
- Replace opaque invented compounds such as `exact-head checks` or `editorial-row layouts` with a description of the actual operation or arrangement. Preserve conventional compounds and correctly formed new technical terms when defined and necessary. Hyphenation alone is not a defect.

## Mechanics

When describing a particular study's methods or results with the researchers as the subject, consider the narrative form `Surname et al. [number] reported …`. Verify the surname, author order, and correspondence to the reference number; this is a pattern, not a source to copy. Write `et al.`, never `et. al.`. Follow the venue's author-count rules, typography, and citation placement, including cases requiring one author or two named authors. Numeric citations remain sufficient without names when attribution is clear. Do not routinely append titles, institutions, or years; include the year when required by the citation system or useful to the argument.

Follow venue rules for heading case, serial commas, quotation marks, hyphenation, en dashes, em dashes, abbreviations, numbers, and units. Otherwise preserve a consistent existing document practice. No single punctuation mark, watched word, polished sentence, or formal construction proves AI authorship or constitutes an editing defect by itself.

## Claims, limitations, and future work

Keep modality proportional to the evidence. Distinguish carefully among `may`, `suggests`, `is associated with`, `supports`, and `causes`; do not convert correlation, partial evidence, or exploratory results into causation or certainty. State which inference a limitation affects, rather than appending a generic disclaimer. Future work should identify the missing data, comparison, experiment, mechanism test, or deployment context needed to address the unresolved issue.

Do not ban passive voice, formal vocabulary, participial clauses, triads, dashes, curly quotation marks, title case, first person, or hedging.
